- **cl_alc_early_drinking_later_problems** · grant_1997 · partly-supported · overstatement: One phrase overstates the source: 'conduct problems, other psychiatric disorders, family environment and other drug use were not measured'. This paper shows only that these were not used or adjusted for. The survey did measure other drug use: the AUDADIS reliability study it cites covered the alcohol and drug modules (p. 109, references), and the claim itself sends readers to the same survey's analysis of other drugs (Grant & Dawson, 1998). Suggested wording: 'were not taken into account in this analysis'. The rest of the claim's account of this source is accurate and appropriately hedged. — Supported from this source: NLAES 1992, 42,862 adults interviewed once, about 92% of selected households and 97.4% of selected respondents (p. 104); 27,616 current (18,352) and former (9,264) drinkers (p. 106); the onset question excluded tastes or sips (p. 105); Table 1 Total column all 14 values with the three SEs quoted, read on the rendered page (40.6, 47.3, 40.8, 38.7, 30.6, 24.5, 16.6, 16.5, 11.4, 10.0, 9.5, 14.7, 13.6, 7.9); abuse without dependence 8.3–13.8% at 16 or younger, peak 13.8% at 14, 2.5% at 25 or older (Table 2); adjusted odds 14% and 8% lower per year with the six covariates as listed, and no ORs, CIs, p values or unadjusted estimates (pp. 106, 108); the editors' conversion to about 0.86 and 0.92 is right; the authors' description of onset age as a powerful predictor and of ages 16 and younger as a particularly vulnerable period (p. 108), and the closing paragraph's statement that the delay itself or, more likely, other associated factors may explain the link, with a call for a prospective study (p. 109). Inferences that are reasonable but not stated in the paper: 'often decades later' (respondents were 18 to over 65; the age distribution of drinkers is not given), and the exclusion of people who had died or were homeless (the paper says household sampling of the noninstitutionalized population of the contiguous US and DC). The point that two adjustments may follow from the disorder is supported by the definitions on pp. 105–106. One more point the claim could add: duration of drinking is age at interview (or last drink) minus age at onset, so adjusting for both age group and duration leaves the onset term partly tied to duration; the authors themselves say onset and duration still need to be disentangled (p. 109). No dosing or advice content in the claim. The paper's own suggestion to delay drinking until after 18 or 19 (pp. 108–109) is correctly left out of the claim.
