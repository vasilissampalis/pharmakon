## cl_mix_benzodiazepines_alcohol: very low, single study, draft

Rests on Koski et al. (2003), Finnish death records 1995–2000, and Tanaka (2002), a one-author narrative review, both read in full (tables rendered; Koski's Figure 1 at 300 dpi).

The median blood alcohol after death was 2.5‰ in 57 deaths with alcohol and temazepam, against 3.3‰ in 615 deaths from alcohol alone. Table 3's 0.8 (24%) is a point estimate made by an unnamed method; its interval is only plotted (about −1.0 to −0.6). It was lower with more temazepam (2.1 against 2.9).

Why very low: observational evidence, down 2 for risk of bias. Only deaths were counted, so no risk can be computed. The groups were not adjusted and differ in age, sex and intent (no suicides among alcohol-only deaths; 11 of 57 with temazepam). No upgrade for the split by concentration: deliberate overdoses may sit in the higher half. The review's "synergistically depress respiration" has no data behind it, and Koski calls the combined effect "generally considered additive"; both are reported, not rated. Alcohol changed nothing in 5 of 8 pharmacokinetic arms, against the abstract's "delayed" metabolism. Koski's abstract's 53% is unused (Table 2: about 13%).

Xanax: alprazolam appears only in one pharmacokinetic arm and in 3 of the review's 39 published deaths, each with other drugs (the name from the identity record). I worded the question "Is it dangerous to mix benzos like Xanax with alcohol?" because the death records concern temazepam; the editor may prefer the brief's wording.

Not read (no DOIs printed): Koski, Ojanperä & Vuori (2002), Alcohol Clin Exp Res 26, 956–959 (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide); the review's citations on breathing (Scavone et al., 1986; Linnoila et al., 1990, Eur J Clin Pharmacol 38, 371–377; Dorian et al., 1985) and its alprazolam study (Linnoila et al., 1990, Eur J Clin Pharmacol 39, 21–28).

## Benzodiazepines with cocaine or stimulants: no claim

Bushnell et al. (2022) analyses the two kinds of overdose separately, never the drugs together. No study on file answers this part, the most-read of the three (616 K and 421 K views; alcohol 97 K). Needed: laboratory studies of both drugs together, or records counting everyone dispensed both.
