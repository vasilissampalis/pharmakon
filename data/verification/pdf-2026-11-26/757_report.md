**10 `cl_benzo_liking` (section 5): very low, replicated by the same team.** The only primary study is de Wit et al.
(1986). In it, 36 volunteers without problem drug use mostly chose the placebo: 21 and 27 of 36
took diazepam at most once at 5 and 10 mg. Diazepam
calmed and sedated them, the euphoria scale did not move, and anxious volunteers chose like the others. Who does choose
benzodiazepines comes second-hand from Griffiths & Weerts (1997): people with problem drug use, moderate drinkers, and
anxious people allowed to take capsules as needed. "Who" compares kinds of people, mostly across studies, so the rating
starts low: −1 risk of bias (the populations got different doses and procedures), −1 indirectness (second-hand, old
laboratory studies), −1 imprecision. De Wit's finding alone would be moderate.

**11 `cl_benzo_misuse_reasons` (section 8): low, single study.** From Maust et al. (2019), US survey 2015–2016: 2.2 in
100 adults misused a benzodiazepine. Their main reasons were relaxing (47.1%), sleep (26.6%) and getting high (11.6%), and
68.8% got the pills from a friend or relative. National probability sample: not downgraded. The reasons
add to 103.6%, and the paper does not say why. Rounding seven figures can account for 0.35 points at most, and the paper
does not say whether more than one main reason was allowed. The abstract's advice on
patients prescribed opioids or stimulants is left out, because Table 2 contradicts it (prescribed opioids AOR 0.69).
