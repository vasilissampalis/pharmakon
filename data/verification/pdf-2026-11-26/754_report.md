## cl_dc_pill_logo: very low, single study

Rests on Vrolijk et al. (2017: 471 online reports against DIMS laboratory records, Netherlands, 2014–2015), Brunt & Niesink
(2011: look-alike tablets holding 2C-B or amphetamine, no counts; identification by look), Vogels et al. (2009: identification
by look; 33,006 laboratory-analysed tablets, 1993–2008), and Brunt et al. (2017) only for its "very reliable", which cites
exactly those two DIMS papers.

Descriptive, starts low. Risk of bias −1: no count of reports found, excluded or unmatched; no rule for multiple matches; no
scorer agreement; reference matched by description, not the same tablet; the reference's own team wrote all four papers.
Indirectness −1: nobody measured how tablets sharing a logo differ; a mismatch mixes wrong reports with look-alike tablets
(the editors' reading, labelled); Dutch only. Not rated as test accuracy. Single study: the DIMS papers are one system's
successive reports, not replications.

Checked as asked: overstatement is the commonest error (39.7 against 17.6 in 100) and was not scored as dangerous; 15.3 in
100 "dangerous" (10.2 understated by over 40 mg, 7.0 a missed psychoactive substance). "Twice as likely" is the odds ratio:
34 of 159 (21.4%) against 38 of 312 (12.2%) is the only split reproducing χ² 6.890; ratio of proportions 1.76, odds ratio
1.96 = 1/0.511. The 99% has no data in either paper.

Would change it (not on file, no DOI printed):
- Cole, J. C., Bailey, M., Sumnall, H. R., Wagstaff, G. F., & King, L. A. (2002). The content of ecstasy tablets:
  Implications for the study of their long-term effects. *Addiction, 97*, 1531–1536. The only source cited for how much
  look-alike tablets vary (Vrolijk's list words the title "contents … long time effects").
- Johnston et al. (2006), *International Journal of Drug Policy, 17*, 464–472 (how people judge pill content).
- The recognition list's validation data (unpublished).
