# Currency check of the 36 methamphetamine claims

Reader: AI reader 8 (currency check, PubMed E-utilities in the app's browser). Searched on 2026-10-16. Read-only: no project file was changed.

## Summary

- Newer evidence that could change the conclusion (yes): 11 claims. Newer evidence that may refine or qualify the claim (maybe): 18 claims. Nothing newer that bears on the question: 7 claims.
- Claims with a yes candidate: 13 (acute_toxicity), 14 (psychosis_treatment), 15 (dependence_baserate), 19 (cognition_normal_range), 23 (psychosis_association), 25 (psychosis_course), 26 (psychosis_vs_schizophrenia), 29 (violence), 30 (us_trends), 34 (bupropion_modafinil), 36 (mirtazapine).

## How the check was done and how to read it

- Search: PubMed E-utilities from a tab I created, at most one request per second, 1 to 5 queries per claim (methamphetamine in title/abstract or MeSH, sometimes with amphetamine terms, plus topic terms and study-design filters). Queries are listed in out.json with the date filter written as a [dp] range; they were sent with datetype=pdat, mindate and maxdate, which is equivalent.
- Window: from the later of the newest cited year and 2018 to the end of 2026. Claims 18, 20 and 33 already cite a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis (Cao 2026), so only 2026 publications were searched for them.
- Screening: titles of the first 20 to 30 hits per query (sorted by relevance), then abstracts of the shortlisted papers. No full text was read, no PDF or download was opened, and no CAPTCHA or bot check was met.
- hits: the sum of the PubMed counts over the queries run for the claim; a paper found by two queries is counted twice, and only the top 20 to 30 per query were screened.
- Duplicates: every candidate was compared by DOI with the DOIs cited by its own claim; none matches.
- could_change_claim: yes means a newer or better source (systematic review, meta-analysis, randomized trial, large cohort or current surveillance data) on the same question that would likely change the claim's numbers, wording or certainty if read and appraised; maybe means newer and relevant but narrower, of similar design, partly overlapping or needing a read to tell, so it could refine or qualify the claim; no means corroborating, tangential or only a better citation.
- Caveats: judgments rest on titles and abstracts. Years are PubMed publication years, which can differ from the online year (for example Boden 2023, Hides 2021, Shirley 2025). PubMed tags Hartikainen 2023 and McKetin 2023 as randomized trials, but they are register and panel-cohort analyses, so they are typed as cohort. Hicks 2023 has no DOI in PubMed.

## 1. Newer evidence that could change the conclusion (yes)

### Claim 13, cl_meth_acute_toxicity: features of acute toxicity (newest cited 2009; 75 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Sibanda 2019, Issues in mental health nursing, PMID 31025889, doi 10.1080/01612840.2018.1553003 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis (searches 1990 to 2017) of the clinical characteristics and risk factors of emergency presentations with methamphetamine intoxication or dependence. It is a systematic, quantitative source for how often clinical features occur in emergency presentations, which the claim says is unknown and currently takes from one narrative review's abstract.
- **maybe**, Isoardi 2019, Emergency medicine Australasia, PMID 30592564, doi 10.1111/1742-6723.13219 (cohort). Retrospective series of 378 emergency presentations (329 people) with methamphetamine toxicity from one hospital, describing management and complications. It gives primary frequencies (behavioural disturbance in 78% and hyperthermia in 5% of presentations) that could replace the claim's unquantified description, though from a single centre.
- **maybe**, McCutcheon 2025, Addiction, PMID 39829384, doi 10.1111/add.16765 (cohort). Observational study of 431 emergency department patients linking blood methamphetamine concentration to the clinical picture. It reports frequencies of psychosis and sedation need and a concentration-to-symptom analysis, relevant to the claim's open question of which features track severity.
- no, Hicks 2023, Emergency medicine practice, PMID 37877728, no DOI in PubMed (other). It is a newer review at the same evidence level as the one cited and a practice summary, not a source of new frequencies.
- no, Fries 2025, Deutsches Arzteblatt international, PMID 40991352, doi 10.3238/arztebl.m2025.0158 (other). It sits at the same level as the cited review and is not specific to methamphetamine.

### Claim 14, cl_meth_psychosis_treatment: treatment of methamphetamine psychosis (newest cited 2018; 55 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Srisurapanont 2021, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 33385693, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108467 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and network meta-analysis of six randomized trials (395 people) of antipsychotics for methamphetamine psychosis. It replaces the claim's narrative-review inventory with a pooled comparison across six drugs (low-quality evidence, aripiprazole doing worse than several others), so the trial count, the drugs compared and the efficacy wording would change.
- **yes**, Fluyau 2019, Frontiers in psychiatry, PMID 31681046, doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00740 (systematic review). Systematic review of trials of antipsychotics for amphetamine psychosis, with searches to November 2018. It is a systematic source on the same question and newer than the three narrative reviews the claim relies on, so it could replace them for the trial inventory.
- **yes**, Chung 2026, Frontiers in pharmacology, PMID 42656845, doi 10.3389/fphar.2026.1892182 (RCT). Randomized trial over 24 months of aripiprazole or paliperidone versus treatment as usual for stimulant-induced psychosis. It is a usual-care-controlled comparison with long follow-up in which aripiprazole did better than usual care on clinician-rated severity at 12 and 24 months and paliperidone did not, which bears on the claim's statement that no controlled evidence exists beyond abstinence.
- **yes**, Hall 2026, The Australian and New Zealand journal of psychiatry, PMID 42844571, doi 10.1177/00048674261490027 (systematic review). Systematic review of the optimal drug, dose and duration of antipsychotic therapy after amphetamine- and cannabis-induced psychosis. It concludes that antipsychotics reduced symptoms but that no regimen was superior and that no data exist on relapse after stopping, which bears directly on the claim's open question about benefit beyond abstinence.
- **maybe**, Wang 2020, Frontiers in psychiatry, PMID 32296355, doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2020.00237 (RCT). Randomized trial of paliperidone extended-release versus risperidone in Chinese patients with methamphetamine-associated psychosis. It found similar efficacy and fewer movement side effects with paliperidone, and because paliperidone extended-release is among the drugs in the network meta-analysis above it is probably one of that analysis's six trials, adding a head-to-head comparison to the claim rather than a new kind of evidence.

### Claim 15, cl_meth_dependence_baserate: proportion of people who use who become dependent (newest cited 1994; 44 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Marel 2019, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 30439610, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2018.10.010 (other). Population-survey analysis of the conditional probability of progressing from first use to a use disorder for alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, sedatives and opioids. It gives a quantified, more recent estimate (lifetime cumulative probability of a stimulant use disorder about 50% among people who used stimulants, above the 37.5% for alcohol) that reverses the ordering reported by the 1994 survey, although stimulants are pooled and methods differ.
- **maybe**, Yimsaard 2018, Journal of addiction medicine, PMID 29176447, doi 10.1097/ADM.0000000000000371 (other). Study of 991 people who use methamphetamine describing age at first use and the time from first use to dependence. It reports a time lag from first use to dependence of about 3 to 5 years, a methamphetamine-specific timing figure the claim lacks, but it gives no proportion of people who go on to become dependent.

### Claim 19, cl_meth_cognition_normal_range: test scores within the normal range (newest cited 2013; 18 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Dean 2018, Neuropsychopharmacology, PMID 29704001, doi 10.1038/s41386-018-0065-1 (other). Case-control study of 37 adults with methamphetamine dependence and 41 controls that used childhood academic transcripts as a premorbid proxy when interpreting cognitive test scores and cortical thickness. It addresses the claim's stated gap (a decline from a higher starting point that no study measured) by comparing current performance with childhood performance, so the caveat in the claim would need rewriting.
- **maybe**, Shirley 2025, Applied neuropsychology. Adult, PMID 36668907, doi 10.1080/23279095.2022.2164197 (other). Study of 241 adults with and without methamphetamine dependence comparing rates of cognitive impairment under two sets of demographically corrected norms. It shows that the proportion classed as impaired depends on the norms used, which refines the claim's figures for the minority meeting impairment criteria.
- **maybe**, Ding 2026, Journal of affective disorders, PMID 41494541, doi 10.1016/j.jad.2025.121144 (other). Cross-sectional study of cognitive impairment in male patients with methamphetamine dependence (32.0% versus 8.3% in healthy controls) and the moderating role of childhood trauma. It supplies another impairment-prevalence figure that can be set beside the claim's 34% versus 16%, plus a childhood-trauma moderator the claim does not consider.
- **maybe**, Guerin 2023, Neuroscience and biobehavioral reviews, PMID 37678571, doi 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105380 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of health, functional and cognitive outcomes in young people aged 10 to 25 years who use methamphetamine (66 studies). It names inhibitory control as the cognitive domain most reliably affected in young people, which adds domain-level context, although the claim concerns adults.

### Claim 23, cl_meth_psychosis_association: association with psychosis (newest cited 2019; 110 hits; window 2019 to 2026)

- **yes**, Boden 2023, Psychological medicine, PMID 34134802, doi 10.1017/S0033291721002415 (cohort). New Zealand birth-cohort study of methamphetamine use and psychotic symptoms across five waves, adjusting for confounders including cannabis use. It is a general-population longitudinal cohort, unlike the treatment and criminal-justice samples behind the claim's odds ratio, with a modest adjusted association (odds ratio 1.33) concentrated in at least weekly use, which would modify both the size and the 'which came first' caveat.
- **yes**, Huang 2022, Evidence-based mental health, PMID 35165118, doi 10.1136/ebmental-2021-300300 (cohort). Ten-year retrospective cohort comparing people with illicit amphetamine records to people without illicit drug records for incident psychosis. It reports about a five-fold higher risk (5.28) with lower risk after rehabilitation, giving follow-up data the claim's mostly cross-sectional pool lacks, though a comparison group without drug records probably overstates the difference.
- **maybe**, Hides 2021, The British journal of psychiatry, PMID 31950892, doi 10.1192/bjp.2019.263 (cohort). Prospective cohort of about 200 people who regularly use methamphetamine testing the direction of the relationship between use and positive psychotic symptoms. It finds a bidirectional relationship of similar size in each direction, which bears on the claim's caveat that most studies cannot show whether use came first.
- **maybe**, McKetin 2023, Addiction, PMID 37157055, doi 10.1111/add.16230 (cohort). Longitudinal panel analysis of methamphetamine use, family history of psychosis and psychotic symptoms in people with methamphetamine dependence. It reports a relative risk of 2.3 for psychotic symptoms in weeks of use and an added role for family history, which supplies vulnerability evidence for the association claim.

### Claim 25, cl_meth_psychosis_course: course and persistence of psychosis (newest cited 2018; 46 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Murrie 2020, Schizophrenia bulletin, PMID 31618428, doi 10.1093/schbul/sbz102 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of 50 studies (40,783 people) of transition from substance-induced, brief and atypical psychoses to schizophrenia. It gives a pooled estimate from primary studies (25%, 95% CI 18% to 35% for substance-induced psychosis) with moderators, against which the claim's second-hand range of 5% to 39% can be checked and replaced.
- **yes**, Voce 2019, Substance use & misuse, PMID 30693832, doi 10.1080/10826084.2018.1521430 (systematic review). Systematic review of 94 articles (7,387 people) on the symptom profile and course of methamphetamine-associated psychosis. It reports a median of 25% of participants with psychotic symptoms persisting beyond one month, a systematic check on the claim's second-hand persistence figures.
- **maybe**, Starzer 2018, The American journal of psychiatry, PMID 29179576, doi 10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.17020223 (cohort). Register-based cohort of conversion to schizophrenia or bipolar disorder after substance-induced psychosis. It reports 32.2% overall conversion with the highest rate after cannabis, and the amphetamine-specific figure has to be read before comparing it with the claim's 19% to 30% register range.
- **maybe**, Ellilä 2025, Addiction, PMID 39933995, doi 10.1111/add.70014 (cohort). Nationwide register-linkage study of relapse within two years after substance-induced psychosis (7,320 people). It reports 20.0% relapse requiring hospital care within two years, mostly of the same substance type, which informs the claim's statement on recurrence.
- **maybe**, Ricci 2026, Schizophrenia research, PMID 41881716, doi 10.1016/j.schres.2026.02.022 (systematic review). Systematic review of 27 studies (about 33,500 people) on predictors of treatment response and transition to chronic psychotic illness after substance-induced psychosis. It is a recent synthesis of predictors of persistence and transition (acute onset, negative symptoms, premorbid functioning) that could add nuance to the course claim, but it is not specific to methamphetamine.

### Claim 26, cl_meth_psychosis_vs_schizophrenia: methamphetamine psychosis compared with schizophrenia (newest cited 2018; 44 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Cohen-Laroque 2024, Schizophrenia research, PMID 38554698, doi 10.1016/j.schres.2024.03.037 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 studies (624 people with methamphetamine-induced psychosis, 524 with schizophrenia) comparing positive and negative symptoms. It replaces the claim's second-hand narrative with pooled estimates (positive symptoms no different, standardized difference -0.01; negative symptoms milder, -0.35), which largely confirms but quantifies and firms up the claim's wording.
- **maybe**, Semancikova 2026, Neuropsychology review, PMID 41941039, doi 10.1007/s11065-025-09687-1 (meta-analysis). Meta-analysis of comparative studies of cognition in substance-induced psychotic disorders versus schizophrenia spectrum disorders. It finds broadly similar cognitive impairment with a small executive-function difference, relevant to the claim's cognition statements, but the studies are not limited to methamphetamine.
- **maybe**, Gicas 2022, Psychiatry research, PMID 34979380, doi 10.1016/j.psychres.2021.114361 (systematic review). Systematic review of cognitive functioning in substance-induced psychosis (searches to December 2020), summarized by substance including methamphetamine. It adds primary-study coverage of cognition in methamphetamine psychosis that the claim reaches only through one narrative review.
- no, Ahmadkhaniha 2022, Basic and clinical neuroscience, PMID 36457876, doi 10.32598/bcn.2021.2837.1 (other). It is a small single-centre comparison consistent with the claim's pattern, adding a data point and not a change.
- no, Eslami Shahrbabaki 2022, Addiction & health, PMID 37559792, doi 10.34172/ahj.2022.1143 (other). It is a small single-centre study with broadly similar impairment in the two patient groups, adding a data point and not a change.

### Claim 29, cl_meth_violence: association with violence (newest cited 2019; 51 hits; window 2019 to 2026)

- **yes**, McKetin 2020, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 32916517, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108262 (cohort). Within-person analysis of crime in months with and without methamphetamine use in an Australian longitudinal cohort. Violent crime was more likely in months of use after adjustment (adjusted odds ratio 3.4, 95% CI 1.5 to 8.0), longitudinal evidence that conflicts with the claim's unclear association for violent offending and its single-study basis.
- **yes**, Foulds 2020, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 31927159, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2019.107826 (cohort). New Zealand birth-cohort study of methamphetamine use and violence perpetration and victimisation up to age 35. It found adjusted odds of about 1.6 for violence perpetration and a dose-response with at least weekly use, which runs against the claim's non-significant pooled adjusted estimate of 1.4 and would change its 'not established' wording.
- **maybe**, O'Malley 2022, Neuroscience and biobehavioral reviews, PMID 35926727, doi 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104805 (systematic review). Systematic review of 28 drug-administration studies (1,069 participants) of acute amphetamine or methamphetamine doses and aggression. It found no acute increase in aggression after single doses in people with limited use histories, an experimental angle that complements the observational association.
- **maybe**, Cumming 2020, Drug and alcohol review, PMID 32212214, doi 10.1111/dar.13062 (systematic review). Systematic review of nine studies of methamphetamine use and health and criminal justice outcomes. It links methamphetamine use before criminal justice contact to later recidivism and drug-induced psychosis, adding criminal justice outcomes but no violence-specific estimate.
- **maybe**, Guerin 2023, Neuroscience and biobehavioral reviews, PMID 37678571, doi 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105380 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of outcomes in young people who use methamphetamine (66 studies). It found that young people who use methamphetamine were more likely to have justice-system involvement and to perpetrate violence, a newer pooled source in a younger population than the claim's.

### Claim 30, cl_meth_us_trends: US use and overdose-death trends (newest cited 2021; 316 hits; window 2021 to 2026)

- **yes**, Zhu 2025, Substance use & misuse, PMID 40509756, doi 10.1080/10826084.2025.2516735 (other). National analysis of methamphetamine- and cocaine-involved overdose deaths in the United States, 1999 to 2023. It extends the death series four years beyond the claim's 2019 endpoint (methamphetamine-involved deaths rising from 547 in 1999 to 34,855 in 2023), so the claim's death figures are out of date.
- **yes**, Tanz 2025, MMWR. Morbidity and mortality weekly report, PMID 40875496, doi 10.15585/mmwr.mm7432a1 (other). CDC report on overdose deaths involving stimulants from January 2018 to June 2024, with and without opioids. It shows 59.0% of overdose deaths involved stimulants and 15.9% involved stimulants without opioids in 2021 to 2024, updating the claim's opioid co-involvement and non-opioid trend.
- **maybe**, Jin 2026, Addictive behaviors reports, PMID 42701355, doi 10.1016/j.abrep.2026.100743 (other). Analysis of US overdose deaths involving stimulants without opioids through 2024. It is the newest data point (methamphetamine-without-opioid death rates up about 2.4% from 2023 to 2024 while total overdose deaths fell) and extends only the non-opioid part of the claim.
- **maybe**, Joe 2026, Journal of addiction medicine, PMID 41985193, doi 10.1097/ADM.0000000000001697 (other). National survey analysis of sex-specific trends in past-year methamphetamine use in the United States, 2002 to 2022. It extends the use-prevalence series to 2022 but reports percentages by sex and not the claim's counts of adults aged 18 to 64, so the 2015 to 2019 figures are not directly updated.
- **maybe**, Jones 2022, Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, PMID 34561865, doi 10.1111/nyas.14688 (other). Review of the epidemiology of methamphetamine use in the United States with implications for prevention, treatment and harm reduction. It is a newer secondary source on US trends that could serve as a cross-check, but it adds no primary survey data beyond those already cited.

### Claim 34, cl_meth_bupropion_modafinil: bupropion and modafinil meta-analysis (newest cited 2016; 94 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **yes**, Bakouni 2023, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 37979478, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2023.111018 (meta-analysis). Meta-analysis of eight placebo-controlled trials (1,239 people) of bupropion for amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder. It is larger and more recent than the claim's five-trial pool and found a small reduction in use (risk ratio 0.90, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.96) and in craving, where the claim reports no difference (odds ratio 0.97), so the bupropion conclusion would need rewriting, with very-low-quality evidence.
- **maybe**, Elkrief 2024, Canadian journal of psychiatry. Revue canadienne de psychiatrie, PMID 39033427, doi 10.1177/07067437241262967 (meta-analysis). Meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials (451 people) of modafinil for amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder. It confirms no effect of modafinil on use (risk ratio 0.99) or retention, supporting the claim's modafinil part, and adds a signal of more serious adverse events at higher doses.
- **maybe**, Sharafi 2024, Addiction, PMID 37880829, doi 10.1111/add.16347 (meta-analysis). Meta-analysis of ten placebo-controlled trials (561 people) of methylphenidate or dextroamphetamine for amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder. It pools the stimulant trials that the claim says could not be pooled: craving fell but urine-confirmed use did not fall significantly (risk ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.01).
- **maybe**, Chan 2019, Addiction, PMID 31328345, doi 10.1111/add.14755 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of pharmacotherapy for methamphetamine or amphetamine use disorder. It is broader and slightly newer than the claim's 2016 source and found no effect of antidepressants as a class and low-strength evidence that methylphenidate may reduce use.
- **maybe**, Ezard 2025, Addiction, PMID 39701142, doi 10.1111/add.16730 (RCT). Randomized placebo-controlled trial of lisdexamfetamine for methamphetamine dependence. It found fewer days of use across the treatment phase but only weak evidence at the 13-week primary point, adding to the claim's stimulant paragraph and not to its bupropion or modafinil conclusion.

### Claim 36, cl_meth_mirtazapine: mirtazapine trials in men who have sex with men (newest cited 2020; 57 hits; window 2020 to 2026)

- **yes**, McKetin 2026, JAMA psychiatry, PMID 41920558, doi 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.0159 (RCT). Phase 3 randomized placebo-controlled trial of mirtazapine for methamphetamine use disorder in 339 adults treated in routine clinical practice. It is a larger trial from a different team with 2.2 fewer days of use out of 28 at week 12 (P = .02), no effect on secondary outcomes, and more drowsiness and weight gain, so it would change the claim's 'not independent replications' caveat and its certainty.
- **maybe**, Naji 2022, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 35066460, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2022.109295 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of mirtazapine for amphetamine or methamphetamine use disorder that found only two trials (180 people), apparently the claim's own two. It pools them to a risk ratio of 0.81 (95% CI 0.63 to 1.03) at 12 weeks with moderate-certainty evidence, adding no new trial but a pooled estimate whose interval includes no effect.
- **maybe**, Rush 2025, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 40580890, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2025.112769 (RCT). Randomized human-laboratory study of mirtazapine and hypothetical methamphetamine demand. It supports a plausible mechanism (lower demand) for the clinical effect but its outcome is a laboratory behavioural-economic measure and not use.
- **maybe**, Black 2026, Journal of clinical psychopharmacology, PMID 41779519, doi 10.1097/JCP.0000000000002148 (RCT). Phase 1b randomized trial of intravenous methamphetamine in 15 people with and without mirtazapine. It adds safety data on combining mirtazapine with methamphetamine (adverse events similar to placebo, cardiovascular response unchanged) that the claim's safety statement lacks.

## 2. Newer evidence that may refine or qualify the claim (maybe)

### Claim 1, cl_meth_pk_halflife: plasma half-life of about 11 hours (newest cited 2008; 63 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Huang 2020, The Journal of pharmacology and experimental therapeutics, PMID 32198137, doi 10.1124/jpet.120.264994 (other). Physiologically based pharmacokinetic model of how acidic or alkaline urine changes the renal clearance and plasma exposure of methamphetamine and its metabolite amphetamine. The single half-life of about 11 hours may need a qualifier because modelled elimination depends on urine pH, although this is a simulation and not a new human measurement.
- **maybe**, Black 2026, Journal of clinical psychopharmacology, PMID 41779519, doi 10.1097/JCP.0000000000002148 (RCT). Two-site phase 1b randomized placebo-controlled trial of intravenous methamphetamine in 15 people, with and without mirtazapine, with plasma pharmacokinetics measured. It is new primary human pharmacokinetic data after intravenous dosing (mirtazapine moved the time to peak earlier by 0.26 hours and changed no other measure), but its abstract gives no half-life, so it adds route detail rather than replacing the 11-hour figure.

### Claim 3, cl_meth_mechanism_release: mechanism of monoamine release (newest cited 2008; 54 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Reith 2020, Handbook of experimental pharmacology, PMID 31286212, doi 10.1007/164_2019_251 (other). Narrative review of the molecular mechanisms of amphetamines, covering the dopamine transporter, dopamine synthesis, VMAT2 and organic cation transporter 3. It is a more detailed and recent mechanistic source than the 2008 review behind the claim, so it could replace that second-hand anchor and refine the wording that the mechanism is not completely understood.
- no, Paulus 2020, JAMA psychiatry, PMID 32267484, doi 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.0246 (other). It sits at the same evidence level as the cited source and adds no primary pharmacology.

### Claim 4, cl_meth_dat_lower: lower striatal dopamine transporter availability (newest cited 2017; 14 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Proebstl 2019, European psychiatry, PMID 30981746, doi 10.1016/j.eurpsy.2019.03.003 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of in vivo neuroimaging of dopamine markers in people who use stimulants, with methamphetamine analysed as its own group. It is a second, later meta-analysis than the 2017 one the claim relies on and reports lower transporter availability in people who use methamphetamine, so it could firm up the direction and supply another pooled size estimate.

### Claim 7, cl_meth_glucose_metabolism: brain glucose metabolism in abstinent people (newest cited 2013; 16 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Azahar 2026, Brain imaging and behavior, PMID 41826602, doi 10.1007/s11682-026-01132-y (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of 18F-FDG PET brain glucose metabolism in substance dependence (seven studies pooled) that includes methamphetamine among several substances. It finds overall lower glucose metabolism (g 0.92) across substances, which sits uneasily with the claim's higher whole-brain value in one methamphetamine study, but the methamphetamine data probably rest on very few studies.

### Claim 8, cl_meth_metabolism_recovery: recovery of glucose metabolism (newest cited 2013; 12 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Azahar 2026, Brain imaging and behavior, PMID 41826602, doi 10.1007/s11682-026-01132-y (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of 18F-FDG PET brain glucose metabolism in substance dependence (seven studies pooled) that includes methamphetamine among several substances. It reports lower metabolism in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate and thalamus during use, withdrawal and prolonged abstinence across substances, which bears on whether metabolism recovers but is not specific to methamphetamine or a follow-up of the same people.

### Claim 9, cl_meth_dopamine_damage: lasting dopamine neuron damage not established (newest cited 2017; 117 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Persons 2023, The American journal of forensic medicine and pathology, PMID 36826228, doi 10.1097/PAF.0000000000000817 (cohort). Retrospective autopsy cohort of 60 decedents comparing brain histology and immunohistochemistry in people with and without chronic methamphetamine use. It is rare human tissue evidence on neurotoxicity and found no definite pathological change in the hippocampus, which fits the claim that damage is not established, although it is small and exploratory.
- **maybe**, London 2020, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 32570138, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108104 (other). PET study of translocator protein, a marker of glial activation, in recently abstinent people with methamphetamine dependence compared with controls. It found no significant elevation of this neuroinflammation marker, adding another damage-related measure that is not clearly abnormal (an earlier report had found elevation).
- **maybe**, Khoshsirat 2020, Journal of chemical neuroanatomy, PMID 32416129, doi 10.1016/j.jchemneu.2020.101802 (other). Post-mortem study of autophagy and cell-death markers (LC3 and ATG5) in the prefrontal cortex of people with chronic methamphetamine use. It adds human post-mortem signals of neuronal cell death in cortex, not in dopamine terminals specifically, so it could extend the markers discussed but cannot settle lasting loss of dopamine neurons.
- **maybe**, Tabatabaei Mirakabad 2021, Journal of chemical neuroanatomy, PMID 33675952, doi 10.1016/j.jchemneu.2021.101942 (other). Post-mortem study of stress-pathway (NUPR1 and CHOP), autophagosome and apoptosis markers in the striatum of people with chronic methamphetamine use. It adds striatal post-mortem evidence of apoptosis-related changes from a small markers study, which could be cited alongside the claim's post-mortem findings without resolving terminal loss versus down-regulation.
- **maybe**, Proebstl 2019, European psychiatry, PMID 30981746, doi 10.1016/j.eurpsy.2019.03.003 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of in vivo neuroimaging of dopamine markers in people who use stimulants, including methamphetamine. It is a further pooled estimate of lower transporter availability, which the claim already accepts, and like the cited studies it cannot tell nerve-terminal loss from reversible down-regulation.

### Claim 10, cl_meth_grey_matter: grey matter differences on MRI (newest cited 2015; 13 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Mackey 2019, The American journal of psychiatry, PMID 30336705, doi 10.1176/appi.ajp.2018.17040415 (other). Mega-analysis of pooled brain-structure data from 23 laboratories (3,240 people, 2,140 of them with dependence on alcohol, nicotine, cocaine, methamphetamine or cannabis). It is far larger and more uniform than the small MRI studies the claim rests on and reports substance-specific effects, so it could change which regions are described as smaller, but the methamphetamine subgroup has to be read.
- **maybe**, Nie 2020, BMC psychiatry, PMID 32272912, doi 10.1186/s12888-020-02567-3 (other). MRI study of grey-matter volume and cortical thickness in people with long-term abstinence from methamphetamine compared with controls. It found few differences from controls after long abstinence and a link between longer abstinence and larger volumes, which bears on the claim's point that some differences may be temporary.
- **maybe**, Ruan 2018, Progress in neuro-psychopharmacology & biological psychiatry, PMID 28887180, doi 10.1016/j.pnpbp.2017.09.004 (cohort). Longitudinal voxel-based morphometry in people who used methamphetamine, scanned at 6 and 12 months of abstinence, with a drug-naive comparison group. Follow-up scans show regional volumes changing over abstinence (cerebellum greater, cingulate lower), the kind of within-person data the claim lacks.
- no, Luo 2026, Translational psychiatry, PMID 41851085, doi 10.1038/s41398-026-03958-y (other). It centres on impulsivity and genetics rather than the regional volume differences the claim reports.

### Claim 11, cl_meth_acute_lab: acute laboratory effects of intranasal doses (newest cited 2012; 87 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Jones 2022, Behavioural pharmacology, PMID 35438671, doi 10.1097/FBP.0000000000000671 (other). Outpatient double-blind laboratory study of intranasal methamphetamine (30 mg per 70 kg) after oral naltrexone or placebo in people who use psychostimulants non-medically. It adds one more intranasal dosing dataset on subjective and cardiovascular effects (single dose, placebo-pretreatment sessions), which could support or qualify the dose-related pattern but has no dose-response arm.
- no, Hayley 2023, Journal of psychopharmacology, PMID 37313987, doi 10.1177/02698811231179805 (RCT). Oral dosing and an alcohol-interaction design corroborate the expected cardiovascular rise but not the intranasal dose-response in the claim.
- no, Black 2026, Journal of clinical psychopharmacology, PMID 41779519, doi 10.1097/JCP.0000000000002148 (RCT). It is a drug-interaction safety trial by intravenous route in which mirtazapine left the cardiovascular effects unchanged, so it does not alter the intranasal findings.
- no, Mayo 2019, Psychopharmacology, PMID 31165207, doi 10.1007/s00213-019-05276-2 (other). Healthy volunteers and a sex-difference question mean it would not change statements about adults who use regularly.

### Claim 12, cl_meth_acute_cognition: acute cognitive effects of single doses (newest cited 2012; 23 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Roberts 2020, European neuropsychopharmacology, PMID 32709551, doi 10.1016/j.euroneuro.2020.07.002 (meta-analysis). Series of meta-analyses of cognitive performance in healthy adults after acute modafinil, methylphenidate or d-amphetamine versus placebo (47 studies). It gives pooled estimates by cognitive domain for a related amphetamine and could replace the claim's reliance on one group's small studies, although it concerns d-amphetamine in adults not selected for methamphetamine use.
- **maybe**, Kassim 2023, Human psychopharmacology, PMID 36251504, doi 10.1002/hup.2856 (systematic review). Systematic reviews of the acute effects of amphetamine on working memory (19 studies) and other cognitive performance (37 studies) in healthy individuals. It finds mixed effects, which supports the claim's cautious wording on improvement, but it concerns amphetamine in healthy people and not methamphetamine in people who use it.
- **maybe**, Hayley 2023, Journal of psychopharmacology, PMID 37313987, doi 10.1177/02698811231179805 (RCT). Randomized trial of the acute neurocognitive and subjective effects of oral methamphetamine with and without low-dose alcohol. It is a newer randomized measurement of neurocognitive effects that includes a methamphetamine-only condition, by oral route and in a different design from the intranasal studies the claim relies on.
- no, Lyu 2026, Biological psychiatry. Cognitive neuroscience and neuroimaging, PMID 41297882, doi 10.1016/j.bpsc.2025.11.005 (other). It is mechanism work with imaging outcomes rather than behavioural test performance, so it would not change the claim.

### Claim 16, cl_meth_withdrawal: withdrawal symptoms and their time course (newest cited 2010; 159 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Li 2023, Addiction, PMID 36401591, doi 10.1111/add.16093 (other). Narrative review of the evidence on clinical management of psychostimulant withdrawal. It is a newer synthesis that may summarize symptom course and duration across studies and could give the claim's single-ward findings some context, but it needs reading to see what it adds.
- no, Zhao 2021, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 33894459, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2021.108702 (other). It addresses correlates of symptom severity in a different population rather than the time course of symptoms, so it would not change the claim.

### Claim 22, cl_meth_siblings: brain structure in siblings (newest cited 2015; 15 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Ersche 2020, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America, PMID 32541059, doi 10.1073/pnas.2002509117 (other). Resting-state fMRI study of 162 people, including people with stimulant addiction, people exposed to stimulants without addiction, siblings of people with addiction, and controls. It comes from the group behind the sibling study and adds a functional-connectivity design that separates vulnerability from drug exposure, so it could nuance the claim's shared-versus-dependent interpretation.
- **maybe**, Just 2019, Translational psychiatry, PMID 30718492, doi 10.1038/s41398-019-0399-4 (other). fMRI study of reward anticipation in four groups defined by familial risk and stimulant use. It separates familial risk from stimulant use for a functional measure, following the same logic as the sibling comparison, but its stimulant group is not specific to methamphetamine.

### Claim 24, cl_meth_psychosis_dose: dose and psychotic symptoms (newest cited 2018; 13 hits; window 2018 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Ma 2018, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 29715654, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2018.03.023 (cohort). Two-year prospective cohort relating the duration of methamphetamine use to psychotic symptoms in 6-month intervals. It offers a separate cohort showing a duration-related pattern (continued 12 and 18 months of use versus no use gave odds ratios of about 2.8 and 9.9), whereas the claim rests on one cohort.
- **maybe**, Boden 2023, Psychological medicine, PMID 34134802, doi 10.1017/S0033291721002415 (cohort). New Zealand birth-cohort study of methamphetamine use and psychotic symptoms across five waves. It found the excess risk concentrated in people who used at least weekly (adjusted odds ratio 2.85) and none below weekly, a frequency threshold that the claim's cohort could not establish.
- **maybe**, Hides 2021, The British journal of psychiatry, PMID 31950892, doi 10.1192/bjp.2019.263 (cohort). Prospective cohort of about 200 people who regularly use methamphetamine analysing the direction of the relationship between use and psychotic symptoms. Its repeated measures test direction, relevant to the claim's limitation that use and symptoms were measured over the same month.
- **maybe**, McKetin 2023, Addiction, PMID 37157055, doi 10.1111/add.16230 (cohort). Longitudinal panel analysis from the same research group of methamphetamine use, family history of psychosis and psychotic symptoms. It adds weekly-level measures and shows how family history combines with use, which could qualify the dose-related risk the claim reports.

### Claim 27, cl_meth_depression: association with depressive symptoms (newest cited 2023; 77 hits; window 2023 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Duncan 2024, Addictive behaviors, PMID 38394960, doi 10.1016/j.addbeh.2024.107988 (systematic review). Narrative systematic review of associations and temporality between methamphetamine, MDMA or cocaine use and anxiety or depressive symptoms. It found some support for temporality, particularly for methamphetamine and depressive symptoms, which bears on the claim's thin longitudinal base of one independent cohort.
- **maybe**, Duncan 2025, Addiction, PMID 39545452, doi 10.1111/add.16714 (cohort). Longitudinal analysis of anxiety and depression symptoms in a community-recruited cohort of people who use methamphetamine. Within-person symptom changes tracked changes in route of administration and dependence severity, adding a new cohort that measured symptoms over time.
- **maybe**, Greenwood 2025, Addiction, PMID 40088044, doi 10.1111/add.70033 (cohort). Integrative data analysis of four Australasian cohorts of amphetamine use and mental health difficulties from adolescence to young adulthood. After adjusting for 15 confounders, monthly or more frequent amphetamine use in adolescence went with 21% higher risk of later mental health difficulties, a further longitudinal estimate that is not limited to depression.
- no, Baguso 2023, LGBT health, PMID 36301245, doi 10.1089/lgbt.2021.0192 (cohort). It tests mental distress as a predictor of stimulant use, the opposite direction, in one specific population, so it would not change the claim.

### Claim 28, cl_meth_suicidality: association with suicidal ideation and attempts (newest cited 2019; 57 hits; window 2019 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Adam 2026, Frontiers in psychiatry, PMID 41908112, doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1654091 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of 70 entries on suicide and psychiatric disorders among people who use amphetamine-type stimulants. Its pooled prevalences (about 20% suicidality and 17% suicide attempts) are newer but are prevalences within stimulant samples and not adjusted associations, so they supplement rather than replace the claim's odds ratios.
- **maybe**, Crump 2021, Journal of psychiatric research, PMID 34700213, doi 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2021.10.017 (cohort). Swedish national cohort of about 6.9 million adults comparing suicide risk across specific substance use disorders, including amphetamine. It supplies large longitudinal data on suicide deaths adjusted for sociodemographic and psychiatric factors, which the claim lacks, although the outcome is death and not ideation or attempts.
- **maybe**, Lee 2021, Addiction, PMID 33788344, doi 10.1111/add.15501 (cohort). Taiwanese nationwide cohort of people with methamphetamine use disorder giving standardized mortality ratios, including for suicide. It gives a methamphetamine-specific suicide mortality ratio of about 16 to set beside the claim's pooled amphetamine figure of about 12.
- no, Mantinieks 2022, Forensic science international, PMID 35908335, doi 10.1016/j.forsciint.2022.111391 (systematic review). It measures how often stimulants are found among suicides, which is a different quantity from suicide risk among people who use, so it does not change the claim.
- no, Darke 2019, Suicide & life-threatening behavior, PMID 29444345, doi 10.1111/sltb.12442 (other). It is a descriptive case series without a comparison group, so it adds circumstances of death but no risk estimate.

### Claim 31, cl_meth_mortality: all-cause and cause-specific mortality (newest cited 2019; 183 hits; window 2019 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Åhman 2024, Addictive behaviors reports, PMID 38800761, doi 10.1016/j.abrep.2024.100553 (cohort). Nationwide longitudinal register study of mortality and morbidity among people who use amphetamine-type stimulants. It reports a standardized mortality ratio of 12.4 (95% CI 11.3 to 13.6), nearly twice the claim's pooled 6.8, from a large recent national cohort that could shift the all-cause estimate if pooled with the older cohorts.
- **maybe**, Lee 2021, Addiction, PMID 33788344, doi 10.1111/add.15501 (cohort). Taiwanese nationwide cohort of people with methamphetamine use disorder giving all-cause and suicide standardized mortality ratios. It is a methamphetamine-specific cohort from after the claim's 2018 search, with an all-cause ratio of 5.4 near the claim's 6.8 and a suicide ratio of 16.3 above its 12, so it supports the order of magnitude.
- **maybe**, Hartikainen 2023, JAMA psychiatry, PMID 36383348, doi 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2022.3788 (cohort). Swedish nationwide cohort of 13,965 people with amphetamine or methamphetamine use disorder comparing medications with hospitalization and death. It provides contemporary mortality outcomes by treatment, relevant to whether the excess mortality can be modified, but it does not estimate the all-cause ratio against the general population.
- no, Åhman 2025, Frontiers in cardiovascular medicine, PMID 39925980, doi 10.3389/fcvm.2025.1378833 (cohort). It addresses cause-specific morbidity and found no independent association after accounting for co-factors, so it does not change the all-cause figures.
- no, Roberts 2026, Addiction, PMID 41097839, doi 10.1111/add.70215 (other). It counts deaths, mostly involving several drugs, without a population denominator, so it cannot estimate mortality ratios.

### Claim 32, cl_meth_naltrexone_bupropion: naltrexone plus bupropion trial (newest cited 2021; 58 hits; window 2021 to 2026)

- **maybe**, Li 2024, Addiction, PMID 38856086, doi 10.1111/add.16529 (RCT). Extended analysis of urine results from the ADAPT-2 trial across both stages and follow-up weeks 13 and 16. It reports a 27.1% versus 11.4% rise in the probability of methamphetamine-negative urine over 12 weeks (difference 15.8 points), a continuous-outcome view that could supplement the claim's responder-based 13.6% versus 2.5%.
- **maybe**, Amin-Esmaeili 2026, JAMA psychiatry, PMID 42234418, doi 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.1092 (meta-analysis). Meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials of medications for stimulant use disorder (866 people in methamphetamine trials) using reduced use and abstinence as outcomes. It found no significant pooled difference from placebo overall or by stimulant type, which is context for how the single trial's moderate-certainty effect should be read, although naltrexone with bupropion is pooled with other medications.
- **maybe**, Bakouni 2023, Drug and alcohol dependence, PMID 37979478, doi 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2023.111018 (meta-analysis). Meta-analysis of eight placebo-controlled trials (1,239 people) of bupropion alone for amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder. It found a modest reduction in use (risk ratio 0.90, very-low-quality evidence) for the bupropion component alone, relevant to separating the contribution of each drug.
- **maybe**, Bastien 2025, Journal of addiction medicine, PMID 39591619, doi 10.1097/ADM.0000000000001422 (meta-analysis). Systematic review and meta-analysis of five studies (419 people) of naltrexone alone for amphetamine-type stimulant use disorder. It found no difference from placebo for use, retention or craving (low to very-low-quality evidence), so the naltrexone component alone is not supported.
- no, Carmody 2026, Journal of addiction medicine, PMID 40626606, doi 10.1097/ADM.0000000000001533 (RCT). It offers a proof-of-concept treatment-selection rule and leaves the trial's headline effect unchanged.
