# _Language-and-stigma — evidence map and PDF list (v0.1, 2026-10-16)

Cross-cutting topic: the evidence behind METHODS §9. The site asserts that the words used about people who use drugs change what clinicians, the public and people who use drugs themselves do; §9 is a pre-registered editorial rule, so it has to rest on cited evidence rather than on preference. This list assembles the experimental studies of term choice, the observational work on stigmatizing language in clinical records, the systematic reviews of stigma-reduction interventions, and the literature on stigma as a barrier to treatment-seeking. Save each PDF into `PDFs/_Cross-cutting/` with the filename given.

**Size note.** 42 papers. Smaller than the brief's 70–110 target, and that is partly the literature (the experimental term-choice studies number perhaps a dozen worldwide) and partly a search problem: Crossref's bibliographic matcher failed outright on the long natural-language titles this field favours, and the endpoint refused five of nine requests. Four named items from the brief's scope are missing and are listed explicitly under Known gaps.

How this list was built: (1) Crossref bibliographic searches on the pre-registered section queries (`_Language-and-stigma_search-log.md`); (2) Crossref `works?filter=doi:` verification for the landmark papers whose titles the bibliographic index would not match; (3) screening by title and journal (dropped: the gaming- and smartphone-"addiction" stigma literature, predatory-looking venues, treatment-modality systematic reviews that matched only on "systematic review" and "substance use disorder", and comorbidity papers).

**Verification.** Every DOI below was either (a) returned verbatim by a Crossref `works?query.bibliographic=` search in this session with title, container-title, year and first author echoed in the table, or (b) verified by a Crossref `works?filter=doi:` fetch in this session — items 1–4, marked **V**. Two further recalled DOIs were checked and **did not resolve to any Crossref record**; they are excluded and named under Known gaps.

**Language note (METHODS §9).** The source titles below use "abuse", "alcoholic", "addict" and "stigmatizing" as the authors wrote them; several of these papers exist precisely to document that usage. Reproducing a title is not endorsing its terminology.

## Known gaps this list does not yet cover

Four of the five items the brief named for this topic are present (Kelly & Westerhoff 2010 = item 1; Ashford 2018 = item 2; Volkow 2021 = item 4; plus Goddu 2018). The following are **not** in the list and must be added before METHODS §9 is published with citations.

- **Kelly, Saitz & Wakeman, "Language, substance use disorders and policy: the need to reach consensus on an 'addiction-ary'" (*Substance Abuse* 2015/2016).** The recalled DOI `10.1080/08897077.2015.1129669` was checked against Crossref (`works?filter=doi:`) and **returned no record**, so it is wrong and the paper is excluded. Locate it by author search in PubMed (string P3).
- **Botticelli & Koh, "Changing the language of addiction" (*JAMA* 2016).** The recalled DOI `10.1001/jama.2016.13057` was checked in the same call and **returned no record**. Excluded; locate by author search (string P3).
- **Kelly, Dow & Westerhoff, "Does our choice of substance-related terms influence perceptions of treatment need? An empirical investigation with two commonly used terms" (*Journal of Drug Issues* 2010)** — the companion experiment to item 1. Not returned by any search and not verified.
- **Ashford, Brown & Curtis (2019), "'Abusing addiction': our language still isn't good enough" (*Alcoholism Treatment Quarterly*)** and the related 2019 terminology papers by the same group — not returned.
- **van Boekel et al. (*Drug and Alcohol Dependence* 2013), "Stigma among health professionals towards patients with substance use disorders and its consequences for healthcare delivery: systematic review."** This is the field's standard systematic review of professional stigma and its absence from this list is the largest single gap. Not returned by the stigma query, which was dominated by intervention reviews.
- **Broyles et al. (*Substance Abuse* 2014), "Confronting inadvertent stigma and pejorative language in addiction scholarship: a recognition and response"** — the statement most journals' terminology policies derive from.
- **The public-opinion experiments.** Barry, McGinty, Pescosolido & Goldman (*Psychiatric Services* 2014, "Stigma, discrimination, treatment effectiveness and policy: public views about drug addiction and mental illness") and Kennedy-Hendricks et al. (*Psychiatric Services* 2017) measure the policy consequences of public stigma and were not reached.
- **The general stigma theory this field borrows from** — Link & Phelan (*Annual Review of Sociology* 2001, "Conceptualizing stigma"); Corrigan's work on stigma-reduction mechanisms — was not searched, and should be, because the site's §9 claim is ultimately a claim about a mechanism.
- **Greek- and other non-English-language terminology evidence.** None was searched. METHODS §3.3 allows Greek sources to be read directly, and the site is authored in Greece, so whether the English-language findings transfer to Greek clinical terminology is an open and locally important question.
