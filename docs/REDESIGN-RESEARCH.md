# Pharmakon redesign: the evidence

Figures checked at source (undated pages retrieved 23 October 2026); evidence type shows strength; → marks the implication.

**1. Little is read.** Users read ≤28% of words (likely 20%), half only on pages ≤111 words; leaving peaks in the first 10 seconds. Logs: 25 literate users; 2 billion visits. → Most MDMA text goes unread.

Nielsen, J. (2008, May 5). *How little do users read?* Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/

Nielsen, J. (2011, September 11). *How long do users stay on web pages?* Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/

**2. Headings steer scanning.** Unformatted text gets F-shaped scanning; scanning headings, then one section ("layer-cake"), is "by far the most effective". Eyetracking. → Few plain-answer headings.

Pernice, K. (2019, August 25). *Text scanning patterns: Eyetracking evidence.* Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/

**3. Attention stays on top.** 74% of viewing time falls in the first two screens (120 participants, desktop). Phone comprehension matched computers (276 participants); hard texts were slower. Eyetracking; experiment. → Safety essentials on screen 1.

Fessenden, T. (2018, April 15). *Scrolling and attention.* Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/

Moran, K. (2016, December 11). *Reading content on mobile devices.* Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/

**4. Few options; overload is contested.** Working memory holds about 4 chunks, not 7. "Choice overload" averaged "virtually zero" across 50 experiments, with wide variation. Review; meta-analysis. → Groups of at most 4.

Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24*(1), 87–114. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922

Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. *Journal of Consumer Research, 37*(3), 409–425. https://doi.org/10.1086/651235

**5. Extras cost learning.** "Seductive details" can hinder learning; progressive disclosure helps novices, but beyond two levels usability is typically low. Meta-analysis; expert guideline. → History and pharmacology one tap away.

Sundararajan, N., & Adesope, O. (2020). Keep it coherent: A meta-analysis of the seductive details effect. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(3), 707–734. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4

Nielsen, J. (2006, December 3). *Progressive disclosure.* Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/

**6. Reading age 9–11.** NHS: "We aim for a reading age of 9 to 11 years old", sentences "up to 20 words". GOV.UK's current guidance gives no age; "Plain English is mandatory"; in 2022 it said "a 9 year old reading age". Guidelines. → One plain version.

NHS digital service manual. (n.d.). *How we write.* https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/how-we-write

Government Digital Service. (n.d.). *Use clear language.* GOV.UK. https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/

Government Digital Service. (2022, May 5). *Writing for GOV.UK* [Archived]. GOV.UK. https://govdiff.njk.onl/update/2022-05-05T11:26:00+01:00/www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk

**7. Numbers are hardest.** 43% of working-age adults in England lack skills for text-only health materials, 61% with numbers. Low health literacy tracks more hospitalisation and emergency care. Observational; review of 96 studies. → Explain numbers in words.

Rowlands, G., Protheroe, J., Winkley, J., Richardson, M., Seed, P. T., & Rudd, R. (2015). A mismatch between population health literacy and the complexity of health information: An observational study. *British Journal of General Practice, 65*(635), e379–e386. https://doi.org/10.3399/bjgp15X685285

Berkman, N. D., Sheridan, S. L., Donahue, K. E., Halpern, D. J., & Crotty, K. (2011). Low health literacy and health outcomes: An updated systematic review. *Annals of Internal Medicine, 155*(2), 97–107. https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-155-2-201107190-00005

**8. Absolute frequencies.** Natural frequencies beat percentages (SMD 0.69); relative risks look bigger and persuade more. Icon arrays help; "1 in 5" versus "20%" is unsettled. Cochrane (moderate quality); review of small studies. → "3 in 100" plus icon array.

Akl, E. A., Oxman, A. D., Herrin, J., Vist, G. E., Terrenato, I., Sperati, F., Costiniuk, C., Blank, D., & Schünemann, H. (2011). Using alternative statistical formats for presenting risks and risk reductions. *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*, *2011*(3), Article CD006776. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006776.pub2

Zipkin, D. A., Umscheid, C. A., Keating, N. L., Allen, E., Aung, K., Beyth, R., Kaatz, S., Mann, D. M., Sussman, J. B., Korenstein, D., Schardt, C., Nagi, A., Sloane, R., & Feldstein, D. A. (2014). Evidence-based risk communication: A systematic review. *Annals of Internal Medicine, 161*(4), 270–280. https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-0295

**9. Pictures help low literacy most.** Across 54 trials pictures "moderately improved knowledge/understanding and recall", especially with low health literacy, though results varied. Meta-analysis. → Real photos, captioned icons.

Schubbe, D., Scalia, P., Yen, R. W., Saunders, C. H., Cohen, S., Elwyn, G., van den Muijsenbergh, M., & Durand, M.-A. (2020). Using pictures to convey health information: A systematic review and meta-analysis of the effects on patient and consumer health behaviors and outcomes. *Patient Education and Counseling, 103*(10), 1935–1960. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pec.2020.04.010

**10. NHS: fixed order, graded boxes.** Alcohol poisoning (~500 words): "Symptoms…"; "Immediate action required: Call 999 if:"; "Things you can do to help…" (Do/Don't); "Treatment…"; "Causes…"; "Find out more". Care cards grade urgency in three levels, one action each; testers "stopped to read the care cards". Inspection; unpublished testing. → One emergency card, same place.

NHS. (n.d.). *Alcohol poisoning.* https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/alcohol-poisoning/

NHS digital service manual. (n.d.). *Care cards.* https://service-manual.nhs.uk/design-system/components/care-cards

**11. GOV.UK: do less.** Principles: "Do less", "Do the hard work to make it simple". Forms: one question per page (no data cited); step-by-step navigation: numbered, expandable steps. Guidelines. → One question per screen.

Government Digital Service. (2025, April 2). *Government design principles.* GOV.UK. https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles

Government Digital Service. (2018, August 7). *Structuring forms.* GOV.UK Service Manual. https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/form-structure

GOV.UK Design System. (n.d.). *Step by step navigation.* https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/step-by-step-navigation/

**12. FRANK: quick layer, fixed sections.** Ecstasy: "Also called"; warning; "Quick info" (six one-line answers); then "How it looks, tastes and smells", "How do people take it?", "How it feels", "Duration", "The risks", "Mixing", "Addiction", "The law"; help/emergency boxes; ~1,300–1,500 words. Ketamine repeats this. Users distrusted government-looking sites. Inspection; service assessment. → Pharmakon's length, but layered.

FRANK. (n.d.). *Ecstasy.* https://www.talktofrank.com/drug/ecstasy

FRANK. (n.d.). *Ketamine.* https://www.talktofrank.com/drug/ketamine

NHSX. (2020, March 18). *PHE Talk to FRANK live assessment.* Digital Health. https://digitalhealth.blog.gov.uk/2020/03/18/phe-talk-to-frank-live-assessment/

**13. Answer first; sources one tap away.** Wikipedia's lead must "stand on its own as a concise overview"; featured snippets show the answer first; Healthline closes with "The bottom line" (no "Key takeaways" box). Readers clicked references in 0.13% of mobile page views. Guidelines; inspection; logs. → Collapse references.

Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Lead section. (2022, June 19). In *Wikipedia* [Archived]. https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20220619141256/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:LEAD

Google Search Central. (n.d.). *Featured snippets and your website.* https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/featured-snippets

Ghoshal, M. (2019, August 20). *Is MDMA (molly) addictive?* Healthline. https://www.healthline.com/health/addiction/is-molly-addictive

Piccardi, T., Redi, M., Colavizza, G., & West, R. (2020). *Quantifying engagement with citations on Wikipedia* (arXiv:2001.08614). arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08614

**14. Duolingo-style feedback; few swipes.** Feedback improves learning (d = 0.48), explanatory feedback most (d = 0.99). In swipeable carousels only 15.7% touched slide 2. Meta-analysis (435 studies); industry logs. → Explaining quizzes; vital facts first.

Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). The power of feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research. *Frontiers in Psychology, 10*, Article 3087. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087

Peatt, K. (2015, February 9). *An exploration of carousel usage on mobile e-commerce websites.* Smashing Magazine. https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2015/02/carousel-usage-exploration-on-mobile-e-commerce-websites/

**15. Speed keeps people.** Users tolerate about 2 seconds for information retrieval. Google's "53% leave after 3 seconds": source unopenable, unused. Experiment. → Usable in under 2 seconds.

Nah, F. F.-H. (2004). A study on tolerable waiting time: How long are Web users willing to wait? *Behaviour & Information Technology, 23*(3), 153–163. https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290410001669914

**16. Dark patterns work quietly.** Mild ones more than doubled sign-ups (11.3% to 25.8%) without measured backlash; the less educated were more susceptible; confirmshaming worked modestly; aggressive ones angered people. Breaking a streak lowers later engagement. Lasting trust costs: little evidence. Experiments. → Pharmakon's readers are most exposed.

Luguri, J., & Strahilevitz, L. J. (2021). Shining a light on dark patterns. *Journal of Legal Analysis, 13*(1), 43–109. https://doi.org/10.1093/jla/laaa006

Silverman, J., & Barasch, A. (2023). On or off track: How (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions. *Journal of Consumer Research, 49*(6), 1095–1117. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029

**17. Drug-site research is thin.** Searchers spent ≤6.5 minutes per drug site; almost half of 1,214 users could not readily find what they sought, and games were minority wishes; methamphetamine users rated a meth site's usability below professionals'. No task-based test with people who use drugs found. Focus groups; surveys. → Test with them.

Drennan, J., Proudfoot, J., Klein, B., Kavanagh, D. J., Baker, A. L., White, A., Kay-Lambkin, F. J., Connor, J., & Young, R. M. (2011). Assessment of function and clinical utility of alcohol and other drug web sites: An observational, qualitative study. *BMC Public Health, 11*, Article 277. https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-11-277

Klein, B., White, A., Kavanagh, D., Shandley, K., Kay-Lambkin, F., Proudfoot, J., Drennan, J., Connor, J., Baker, A., & Young, R. (2010). Content and functionality of alcohol and other drug websites: Results of an online survey. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 12*(5), e51. https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1449

Kershaw, S., Birrell, L., Deen, H., Newton, N. C., Stapinski, L. A., Champion, K. E., Kay-Lambkin, F., Teesson, M., & Chapman, C. (2021). Evaluation of a digital health initiative in illicit substance use: Cross-sectional survey study. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 23*(8), e29026. https://doi.org/10.2196/29026

## 12 design rules for Pharmakon

1. Home: one-line purpose, search, top drugs (1, 17).
2. Drug pages open with two sentences and "Quick info" (1, 3, 12, 13).
3. Fixed sections, plain-answer headings (2, 10, 12).
4. One graded emergency card, same place (10).
5. Reading age 9–11; sentences under 20 words (6, 7).
6. Two layers; extras in "Deep dive" (5).
7. At most four options per group (4).
8. One step per screen in tools and long topics (3, 11).
9. Risks as "x in 100" with icon arrays (7, 8).
10. Real photos of each form (9).
11. Collapsed references; under 2 seconds to load (13, 15).
12. Explaining quizzes, not streaks, guilt prompts or autoplay; test with people who use drugs (14, 16, 17).
