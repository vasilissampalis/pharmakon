# Writing rules: how every answer on the site is written

Version 4 · 1 November 2026 · applies to every sentence and picture a reader sees.

**Who the site is for.** The senior author, 18 October 2026: "this is a site for the drug users and people interested in drugs. science and facts just back everything up. it's firstly directed for the users and the science is the means to do it right and educate users in the best way possible." Every format choice starts from that reader; the evidence is how we get it right, never the point of the page.

The senior author asked for answers "as if we explain these concepts to an 8 year old", with all the rigour kept, for people who use drugs and for scientists at once, and for a site people enjoy using: "I want my mum to understand this." These rules are how. Every rule rests on a study listed at the end (each description checked against the paper's abstract); nothing on the site is there only to look clever.

## Rule 0: real life, not abstraction

The senior author, 17 October 2026: "our encyclopedia educates users in real life, not abstraction. our website has to talk to all people, and guess what, most of them are regular people and users!"

- **Ask the person's question.** Every answer starts from the question a person who uses the drug, or their family, would ask: what it does and for how long, what makes it risky, mixing (prescribed medicines included), tolerance, the days after, pregnancy, and whether it is addictive.
- **Real-life questions first.** On each drug page, the real-life questions come before the research and regulatory disputes, which stay for the scientists further down.
- **Say what the finding means for a real situation.** Name where the studies differ from real use (laboratory doses, one dose, healthy volunteers) instead of leaving it abstract.
- **Findings only.** Real-life does not mean advice on amounts or timing: the answer gives what studies found, never instructions.
- **"I barely felt it."** The senior author, 18 October 2026: some people feel that an amount does little or nothing, and then take far more. Wherever a page speaks about amounts or effects, say this plainly and without judging, then give what the studies show:
  - why it happens: a slow start, tolerance, a weaker or different product, a medicine that blunts the effect, or bodies that differ;
  - what happens when people then take more.
  - This is a finding to know, never advice on how much to take.
- **No copied text.** Every sentence is our own. A quotation is at most 15 words, at most one per claim, word for word and attributed. No figures, tables, photographs or long passages from papers are reproduced: numbers are restated in our own words with their source (METHODS §9).

## Rule 0b: the whole picture, not only the crashes

The senior author, 18 October 2026: "a drug user may google for the drug they like and they would only find addiction deaths and problems about the drug. As Carl Hart said, it's like you like cars but when you google them you only find car crashes. that's a problem we want to fix: giving drug users and people who want to be educated about drugs education that does not vilify drugs but gives realistic facts/feedback/info and has a critical eye not only on the info that is generally online but have a critical eye on academic papers too, like Carl Hart does and every good scientist should do. this philosophy should lead our website's mentality."

- **What it does, good and bad.** Every drug page answers what the drug does that people take it for (the effects people seek, as studies measured them) as plainly as what can go wrong, and near the top. A page that lists only harms is unfinished.
- **Risks in proportion.** Say how many of the people who use a drug run into each problem, out of 100 or 1,000, with the base rate first: most harms happen to some people, not to everyone who uses. Compare with a familiar risk only when a source makes the comparison.
- **The same critical eye on every source.** Online claims, official statements and academic papers are all checked the same way: who took part, the amounts compared with real use, whether there was a "before" measurement, whether it was repeated, who paid, whether it was retracted, and how it was reported. A study that finds harm and a study that finds none meet the same bar. Where a well-known claim does not hold up, say so plainly (the 2002 monkey study, withdrawn a year later).
- **Neither vilify nor sell.** No words of alarm or reassurance; person-first words; pleasure and harm in the same plain voice. Describing the effects people seek is a finding, not encouragement.
- **On the site, in our own words.** The cars-and-crashes idea is said in our own words and not attributed until its source is found and checked.

## Photos

The senior author, 18 October 2026, asked for real photos of the drugs instead of symbols, the same drug in all its forms, and specific rather than vague pictures.

- **What it looks like.** Each drug page shows one photo per common form (pills, powder, crystal, blotter, liquid, plant, medicine): street and shop forms first, then medicines. Seeing varied examples makes recognition hold up on new ones (Raviv et al. 2022).
- **"Yes, that's it."** The senior author, 18 October: the reader has to recognise the drug as they know it, with the familiar feeling of "I've seen this". So each photo shows the form as people meet it, close up, well lit, with nothing else in the frame: no coins, rulers, matches, evidence labels, watermarks, seizure scenes or bags (cut out, or the photo is replaced). A 2 to 4 word label sits under it, next to the picture (Ginns 2006).
- **Every form, and what it is.** Each drug page lists every form people meet (data/site/forms.json): its plain name, one line on what it is and where it comes from, restated from the sourced form records, with their sources, and a photo where a free one exists. Forms without a photo show their symbol until one is found.
- **One example.** Every gallery says that it shows one example, that the same drug can look different and different drugs can look the same, and that looks don't tell what's inside. Medicine photos add that looks differ by maker and country.
- **Look-alikes side by side, like with like.** Different drugs that look alike are shown together (the drug-checking page): loose white powders with loose white powders, pills with pills, so the comparison is the real one. Comparing similar things side by side teaches the difference best, most of all with pictures (Brunmair & Richter 2019).
- **Brands.** Generic and street forms first. A brand appears only as one example of a medicine, or as one of several packets in one photo; brand names go in the text, country by country, so no country is featured.
- **A familiar, safe place.** The site should feel like a place for people who use drugs: no police or seizure imagery, no warning graphics, no scare framing; real photos and plain, friendly words.
- **Never** people, hands, drugs being taken, or kit in use. Drug cues raise craving in people dependent on a drug, strongly (Carter & Tiffany 1999), and such photos could read as instructions.
- **Hide switch.** One tap hides every drug photo and brings the form symbols back; the choice is kept on the reader's device only.
- **No decoration.** A photo goes only where it helps a real decision (recognising a form, a naloxone kit, a test). A picture with nothing to explain it waits for its text (the naloxone kit waits for the Emergency page). Interesting extras that do not carry the answer reduce learning (Rey 2012; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020).
- **Licences.** Public domain, CC0 or CC BY only (CC BY-SA only if nothing else exists, marked). Each photo's credit and licence show under it and in About; crops are marked; metadata (EXIF, GPS) is removed from the web copies. No AI images.
- **Captions describe the photo.** A fact about the drug goes through a checked claim, never through a caption.

## One page, three depths

| Depth | Who it is for | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Quick answer | everyone | the short answer, its "But", a picture of the numbers, the evidence bars, the sources |
| 2. How we know | the curious | two to four short points, written from the plain sentence of the claim |
| 3. Full evidence | scientists, checkers | the precise statement, anchors with page numbers, study cards, GRADE reasons |

A shallower depth never says more than a deeper one. The short answer is written from the claim, never beyond it.

## The answer format, in this order

0. **In short, first** (26 October): each drug page opens with three answers that need no tapping (the first question of each topic, in the editors' order), each with its "But" and its evidence bars. The full evidence for any answer opens over the page, so the reader never loses their place.
1. **A question as the heading**, in the reader's words ("Does meth damage dopamine nerve cells?"). Questions are grouped by topic; a row of topic buttons shows one group at a time.
2. **The short answer**: one or two short sentences, about 14 everyday words or fewer. Target: school grade 6 or below (the build lists every line above it).
3. **The "But" line**: the single most important limit (one study; no "before" measurement; one lab; old survey).
4. **Numbers as counts out of 100** ("11 in 100"), with a picture of 100 dots when a share or a risk is the point; a rare risk is shown out of 1,000 so it is not made to look bigger. Comparisons use the same "out of". Absolute numbers come before relative ones.
5. **Evidence bars** in words (Strong, Moderate, Limited, Very limited evidence) and **who found it** (several teams, one team, one study).
6. **Sources you can see**: a chip "Author Year" with a book icon. Pointing at it shows the full APA 7 reference; clicking opens the article. Every in-text citation carries the same book mark.
7. **How we know**: two to four short points, one tap away (target: grade 8 or below).
8. **Full evidence**: one more tap, in a sheet over the page; Back, Escape or the close button return the reader to the same spot (26 October).
9. **Test yourself** (26 October): one question at a time, in a new random order on every visit, unanswered ones first (kept on the reader's device only). Every question is built from checked data, so its answer is right by construction: true or false and multiple choice written for checked answers; "Which is the answer?" (a page's question and three short answers, two from other topics); "Which has stronger evidence?" (two checked answers rated differently); street names two sources agree on; drug families. On the home page, on every drug page (that drug first) and on its own page.
10. **Small rewards for exploring**: a tick marks each answer the reader has opened, a bar shows how many of a page's answers they have opened, and the home page offers to go back to the last drug page (1 November). All of it is kept only in the reader's own browser; nothing is sent anywhere, and there are no streak warnings, reminders or prizes.
11. **Do home test kits work?** (27 October, the senior author's question of 26 October): the graded answers on test kits
   and drug checking sit on the drug checking page, with their own In short, in two topics (what the tests find, what
   testing changes). Each drug's Test it first card shows the two answers that fit the tests it names, after where to get
   a kit, each opening its evidence over the page (`data/site/kits-for.json`, navigation only). Lab results are always
   called lab results; what people said they did is never called what they did.
12. **One answer leads to the next** (1 November): 'Next question' at the end of each answer opens the next one in its list; an empty search box shows five real questions to try; every answer has a Share button (the phone's own share sheet, or the link is copied).

## Sentence rules

- Everyday words. Swap a technical word for a plain one ("dummy pill", not "placebo") or explain it once.
- One idea per sentence; short sentences; active voice.
- Talk to the reader where it fits; plain, not chatty.
- The same word for the same thing every time.
- No extras: no jokes, anecdotes, decorative pictures or "fun facts" that do not carry the answer.
- Words sit next to the picture or number they explain.
- Say plainly what is not known, and give ranges as numbers.
- "Linked to" unless the studies can show "causes".
- Person-first words ("people who use meth"); no words of alarm or reassurance.
- A question heading has 12 words or fewer (1 November; the 24 longer ones were shortened).
- The key number of a short answer is shown in bold, so the eye finds it first (done by the site; years are skipped).
- Every country is equal: no country is featured. Lists run world, then regions, then countries in alphabetical order.
- Emergency help is one menu item and one quiet line where risks are discussed, not a banner.

## Checks before a line goes live

- Reading level: the build lists every short line, "But" line and True-or-false statement above grade 6, and every How-we-know point above grade 8. Drug and medicine names push the score up; when a name is the reason, the line stays.
- A True-or-false statement is built only from its claim's short line; its answer is what the claim shows, and the reveal always carries the "But".
- Menus and lists: about four main choices at a time; the rest under "More".
- It never says more than the claim, and its "But" carries the claim's main limit.
- Could a reader say what it means, and what follows from it? (PEMAT and the CDC Clear Communication Index ask the same.)
- A real reader who does not know the field reads it before the senior author signs off: experts cannot easily un-know what they know.

## Where the text lives

- Short answers, "But" lines, pictures and quiz: `data/site/simple.json` (one entry per claim; written from the claim, for the senior author's review).
- Questions as headings: `data/site/questions.json`.
- Which test-kit answers a drug's Test it first card shows: `data/site/kits-for.json` (navigation only; it asserts nothing).
- Plain and precise sentences: the claim files in `data/claims/`.
- When a claim changes, its `simple.json` entry is rewritten in the same commit.
