    How to read this site

    Every answer has five parts

    1. The short answer. What the research found, in a few plain words.
    2. The catch. The "But" line: the main limit of what we know.
    3. How strong the evidence is. One to four bars. Four bars: more research is very unlikely to change the answer. One bar: we can't be sure.
    4. Who found it. A label says if several teams found the same thing, or only one study.
    5. The sources. Put your mouse on a source, or tap it on a phone, to see the full reference. Click it to open the study.

    Want more? Open "How we know", or switch the page to Full evidence: every study, every number, and where it is in the paper.

    For now, every answer is a draft: our editor has not checked it yet. Each answer says how far its checks have got.

    A real example

    Put your mouse on a source (or tap it), open a study card, or open "Why this rating".

    "Linked to" is not "causes"

    Two things can go together without one causing the other. People who use a drug may differ in other ways too. We say "causes" only when the studies can show it. Most of what we know about drugs in people is "linked to".

    Seven questions to ask about any drug story

    Our study cards answer them for every study. They work on headlines, leaflets and friends' stories too.

    1. Who was studied? People in treatment, in prison or in hospital are not everyone who uses a drug.
    2. Were they tested before they started? If not, a difference may have been there before the drug.
    3. How much, how often, and how? One dose in a lab is not the same as using every day.
    4. How big is the difference? "Significant" means probably not chance. It does not mean big. A group can score a bit lower on average while almost everyone in it scores in the normal range.
    5. Out of how many? "Twice the risk" can mean 2 in 100 instead of 1 in 100, or 2 in a million instead of 1 in a million.
    6. Did another team find the same? One study is a lead, not a fact.
    7. Does the summary say more than the data? Headlines often do. Our study cards note the gap.

    Found a mistake?

    Use the "Ask or report" button, at the bottom right of every page. For a fact, add a source: a DOI, PMID or page number. Every accepted fix is listed in public.

    For scientists: every part of an answer, in full

    The parts of a claim

    1. The plain sentence. What it means for a person who uses the drug, in ordinary words. It never says more than the precise sentence below it.
    2. The precise sentence. Population, exposure, comparator, outcome, and size of the effect, in the words a scientist would accept, with the citations attached.
    3. The evidence bars. How strong the evidence is, on the GRADE scale: four bars (strong evidence) is GRADE's high certainty, three moderate, two (limited evidence) low, one (very limited evidence) very low. Randomized evidence starts at four and observational evidence at two; a study that checks a test against a laboratory method starts at four, as GRADE rates tests; the reasons for every move up or down are under "Why this rating".
    4. The status label. found by several teams at least two independent studies agree; repeated by one team only agreement within one research group, which is weaker; one study one study only; experts disagree credible evidence both ways, with a debate box; results point both ways inconsistent findings. (In the Methods these are called replicated, replicated by the same team, single study, contested and mixed.)
    5. The citation. Tap (or hover) for the full reference and a link to the paper. Every journal article's details were pulled from Crossref and checked for retractions on the date shown; reports and web pages were checked against the publisher's page.
    6. The study card. The paper taken apart: what its design can and cannot show, who was studied, how the drug use was measured, what the comparison group lacked, what the data show versus what the abstract claimed, who paid, and what would settle the question.
    7. Evidence anchors. Where in the paper the claim's numbers are: page, section or table, and the data themselves in our words. We do not copy passages; you can open the paper at that page and check. Every anchor is checked against the source by a second AI reader before a claim is published; the senior author's own checks of each anchor continue after publication, and the check stamp shows how far they have gone.
    8. The check stamp. Under each claim: "Approved by our editor; sources checked by a second AI reader" (the senior author has read and approved the answer at this version, and every anchor was confirmed by a second AI reader), "Sources checked by a second AI reader", "Checked by our editor", "Published" or "Problem reported: being corrected", with the version number. Hover or long-press it for the step the claim has reached on the protocol's verification ladder: DRAFT (not yet on the ladder: an anchor still to be located in a full text, or the claim rests only on narrative reviews), METADATA-VERIFIED (its references confirmed against Crossref and checked for retractions), AI-ANCHORED (every anchor confirmed by a second AI reader: re-found without seeing the first reader's work, or checked at the place the first reader gave), ANCHOR-VERIFIED (every anchor checked against the paper by the senior author), APPRAISED (every study card it rests on read against its source by the senior author), SENIOR-REVIEWED, PUBLISHED, or FLAGGED (a problem reported after publication is open and being corrected). The site shows its own state of completion on every claim.

    Editorial protocol · v{{PROTOCOL_VERSION}}

    Methods

    In short

    1. We search for the best studies on each question, reviews of many studies first, and log every search.
    2. We read each study in full, not just its summary.
    3. We check every study for weak spots, with the same tools whatever it found.
    4. We rate how sure we can be of each answer, on the GRADE scale doctors use.
    5. Three readers check every answer: an AI reader, a second AI reader who works without seeing the first one's work, and then our editor. Nothing is final before our editor has checked it.
    6. When we get something wrong, we fix it in public.

    The rest of this page is the full method, for scientists.

    This is the condensed, public version of the editorial protocol. The full protocol is the authoritative document and is versioned alongside the content. Its first version was written before any monograph; every later change is listed in its changelog with the reason and the date, so readers can judge whether a rule was fitted to a conclusion.

    Principles

    1. Symmetry. Every study is appraised with the same instrument regardless of its conclusion, funder or authors.
    2. Claims, not narratives. A paragraph with no citable claim is deleted.
    3. Anchored evidence. Every claim carries at least one page-located evidence anchor from a source read in full. No claim from an abstract alone. Quotations only where the wording itself is the point, never more than 15 words.
    4. Uncertainty is content. "Not known" is a valid and often correct answer.
    5. Base rates first. Prevalence and absolute risks before relative risks and anecdotes.
    6. The interpretation gap is reported. What the data show is separated from what the abstract and the press claimed.
    7. Corrections are public. Every change to a published claim is versioned and visible.
    8. Interests are declared and never used as a trump card.

    Search

    Per substance: PubMed/MEDLINE, Europe PMC, Cochrane Library, OpenAlex/Crossref, Google Scholar; registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, PROSPERO); monitoring bodies (EUDA, UNODC, WHO, NSDUH, ESPAD). Systematic reviews form the backbone; primary studies are retrieved where no adequate review exists, where a claim is contested, for landmark studies and for work newer than the last review. Critiques and replies on landmark studies are retrieved deliberately. Backward and forward snowballing on every included review and landmark. Every search is logged with date, string, hits and exclusions (PRISMA 2020). Retraction status is checked for every source through Crossref's Retraction Watch data.

    Study cards

    Every supporting study is reverse-engineered from its conclusions into its mechanism: design and what it can and cannot establish; sample and recruitment with selection effects named; exposure measurement including purity and polysubstance use; the comparison group and the premorbid variables that were never measured; outcome measures, multiplicity and blinding; effect sizes with intervals; the interpretation gap; replication timeline; funding; and what would settle the question. Risk of bias uses the instrument that matches the design:

    DesignInstrument
    Randomized trialsRoB 2
    Observational exposure studies (most drug-effect studies)ROBINS-E
    Non-randomized intervention studiesROBINS-I
    Systematic reviews and meta-analysesAMSTAR 2
    Animal studiesSYRCLE
    Prevalence studiesJBI checklist
    Narrative reviewsNo validated instrument: the editors' checklist (search, appraisal, reliance on the authors' own work, evidence versus speculation, balance); never the sole support of a claim above draft
    Qualitative studiesThe editors' qualitative checklist (domains adapted from CASP)
    Reports by public bodies (Surgeon General, IARC, National Academies, government reviews)The editors' checklist for evidence reports (search and selection, appraisal of the studies, stated grading rules, independence and funding, external review, conclusions within the evidence); AMSTAR 2 where the report states a systematic search. Their graded wording is quoted as their judgment, not ours
    Case series, case reports, modellingNo instrument; the study card states the design's limits

    Each instrument keeps its own words (ROBINS-E: low, some concerns, high, very high; RoB 2: low, some concerns, high), and for the tools that build an overall judgment from domains (RoB 2, ROBINS-E, ROBINS-I and the editors' checklists), the overall judgment is never milder than the worst domain. The validator refuses a card that mixes them, and a script checks that the instrument matches the design.

    Three readers. An AI first reader writes the card and the evidence anchors from the full text. An independent AI second reader, given only the claims, finds the evidence for and against them in the paper before it sees the first reader's work, then checks the card. A script matches the two readers' anchors; anchors the blind reading did not re-find, or that were added later, are checked by a second reader at the place given and labelled 'checked at the stated place'. An adjudicating AI reader settles the second reader's findings on each card against the source and logs every change on the card; differences of judgment are left to the senior author and shown. The senior author then checks every anchor against the source, reads every study card against its source, and may overrule any judgment. Before that, AI methodologist reviewers who took no part in the drafting read every claim against its evidence and the protocol.

    Claims and certainty

    A claim is written in a fixed form: in population, exposure compared with comparator is associated with / increases / reduces / causes / does not detectably change outcome: direction and magnitude with interval. Causal verbs (increases, reduces, causes) are used only when the designs support them; descriptive claims (prevalence, trends, death rates) describe and do not explain. Certainty follows GRADE:

    High (shown as strong evidence)Further research is very unlikely to change the estimate.
    ModerateFurther research may change the estimate.
    Low (shown as limited evidence)Further research is likely to change the estimate.
    Very low (very limited evidence)Any estimate is very uncertain.

    Randomized evidence starts high; observational evidence starts low. A study that checks a test's results against a laboratory method starts high, as GRADE rates tests, and is rated down for chosen samples, laboratory conditions instead of real use, one brand or batch, and small numbers; whether testing changes what people do is graded separately, from studies of people. Downgrades for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias; for observational evidence only, upgrades for large effects, dose–response and confounding that would reduce the observed effect. A claim inherits the certainty of its weakest necessary link. "No detectable effect" requires precision, not a non-significant p-value. Relative effects are always accompanied by absolute effects where a base rate is known. A large effect counts only if it survives the most adjusted model with no bias left that could inflate it, and never for a prevalence, a count, a mortality ratio or a trend over time. When evidence already rated down for bias is also rated up, the claim says so and states the lower rating a strict reading would give. Claims that rest only on narrative reviews stay in draft and say their figures are second-hand. Status flags: replicated replicated by the same team single study contested mixed.

    Contested questions

    Any contested claim is presented in six fixed parts: the question; the strongest evidence for yes; the strongest evidence for no; what neither side's evidence can show, by design; the editors' reading, labeled as such, with the certainty rating; and what would settle it. Both sides are written to the standard their own best proponents would accept.

    Two registers

    Every claim is written twice. The plain sentence is for a person who uses the drug and wants the answer; the precise sentence is for anyone who wants to check it. The plain sentence may never say more than the precise one supports.

    Sourced facts

    Names and identifiers, legal status, official statistics and emergency numbers are not graded claims. Each names its source, the date it was read and how: in the app's browser, from a PDF, or through WebFetch, a fetch tool that answers through a summarizing model. A fact read through WebFetch is confirmed in a second source or by a second reader, and every sourced fact stays a draft until the senior author has checked it. Papers are never read through WebFetch.

    Language, statistics, updates

    People who use drugs; dependence and use disorder in their DSM-5/ICD-11 senses with the instrument named; no adjectives of alarm or reassurance. Every statistic carries source, edition year, data year, measure definition, population and link, and is never rounded beyond the source. A full re-search every twelve months, or sooner on a major study, retraction or credible critique; the date of the last search is printed on every page. Corrections go through a form that requires a DOI, PMID or page reference; the editors answer within 14 days, and accepted corrections are logged publicly with what was wrong, what changed and why.

    About Pharmakon

    What this is, and what it is not

    In short

    • For people who use drugs, and anyone interested. It answers the questions people really ask. The science is how we get the answers right.
    • The whole picture, not only the crashes. What a drug does that people take it for, what can go wrong, and how often, in numbers. Most harms happen to some of the people who use a drug, not to all of them, and we say how many.
    • A critical eye on every source. Headlines, official statements and scientific papers get the same questions: who took part, how much they took compared with real life, whether it was repeated, who paid, and whether the study was later withdrawn.
    • It grows one question at a time. Every page says what is checked and what is still uncertain.
    • It is written with AI help (Claude, made by Anthropic; for one week in November 2026 also ChatGPT, made by OpenAI). A second AI reader checks every passage against its source, and our editor, a psychologist and clinical neuropsychologist, reads and approves every answer. Until he has, the answer says it is a draft.
    • It is free to read. It takes no money from alcohol, tobacco, cannabis or drug companies.
    • It explains what research found. It does not encourage anyone to use drugs, says nothing about where to get them, and gives no instructions on amounts, timing or ways of taking them. It is not medical or legal advice.

    Using this information

    Pharmakon describes what published research found. It is information, not medical advice, and not encouragement to use any drug. We check every claim against its sources, but errors are possible: please check important decisions with a health professional, and in an emergency call your local emergency number.

    Privacy

    • Pharmakon has no accounts, no ads, no cookies and no tracking or analytics.
    • A few choices stay on your own device, in your browser's storage, and are never sent to us: the country you pick, whether drug photos are shown, your reading view, which answers you have read, the last drug page you opened (so the home page can take you back) and your quiz progress.
    • The site is a set of fixed pages served by Cloudflare Pages, which, like any web host, receives your device's internet address in order to deliver them.
    • The feedback button sends nothing by itself: it writes an email that you send from your own account, and emails to us are received by our email provider, Zoho Mail (EU).
    • Links to other sites, such as a drug-checking service, take you to pages with their own privacy rules.

      Copyright and the law
      • Our own words. Every sentence here is written by us. We do not copy text, tables, figures or photographs from papers; numbers are restated in our words, with their source.
      • Short quotations only. At most 15 words, at most one per answer, word for word, with the source named, as the quotation exception allows (EU Directive 2001/29/EC, article 5(3)(d); in Greece, Law 2121/1993, article 19).
      • Laws and official texts. Legal status is reported from the official texts, which are not protected by copyright in Greek law (Law 2121/1993, article 2(5)) or in most countries; the official text is always the authority.
      • Official figures. Statistics from public bodies are shown with the body, the edition and the page they come from.
      • Photos. Only photos that anyone may reuse (public domain, CC0 or CC BY), each with its credit and licence.
      • Education, not encouragement. What we give is findings and where they come from, so people can make safer decisions; never advice to use, or how to use.
